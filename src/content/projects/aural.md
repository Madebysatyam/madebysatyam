## Overview

When I joined Pocket FM in December 2023, the platform was already operating at extraordinary scale. More than 200 million users. Hundreds of thousands of hours of original audio content. A payment model pioneering a genuinely new form of entertainment consumption.

But when I looked at the product through a designer's eyes, something didn't add up. Here was a platform at the frontier of an entirely new entertainment category — and its interface could have belonged to almost any other app in the world.

> **"The product had enormous ambition and zero design personality."**

The visual language was borrowed. The interaction design was familiar to the point of being forgettable. That gap between what Pocket FM was becoming and how it presented itself to the world became the problem I decided to spend the next phase of my career solving.

The result is Aural — a multi-sensory design system built not just to govern components and ensure consistency, but to give Pocket FM a recognisable, ownable feeling for the first time in its history. This is the story of how that happened.

## Part 01 — What I Found

### The audit

My first weeks were spent doing something unglamorous but essential: looking at everything. I moved through the product surface by surface — onboarding, discovery, playback, account, purchase flows — and made notes not about what was broken in any individual screen, but about what the cumulative experience felt like.

Design audits that focus on individual components tend to produce lists of fixes. What I wanted was a read of the whole.

---

### Four patterns that kept surfacing

**01 — Visual language**
Generic and interchangeable. Strip the logo and the screen still makes sense as a competitor's.

**02 — Writing**
No consistent voice. Home screen copy felt different from the player, which differed again from transactions.

**03 — Sensory feedback**
Haptics minimal. Motion functional, not expressive. An audio product with an inert interface.

**04 — Accessibility**
Not catastrophic gaps, but enough to show it had been treated as a retrofit, not a foundational constraint.

---

### The pattern behind the patterns

Individually, each issue looked like a design problem with a design solution. Generic illustrations? Commission better ones. Inconsistent writing? Write a tone-of-voice guide. But there was something larger going on.

What the audit was showing me was not a list of disconnected defects. It was evidence of a systemic absence. There was no shared framework defining how the product should feel. Teams were solving local problems well, but there was nothing connecting their work at the level of experience.

> **"Every team had a good answer to their own question. Nobody had asked the larger question."**

The visual, motion, language and interaction layers of the product operated as independent systems. In a product at this scale, that absence compounds over time. Every new feature, every new surface, every new market added to the divergence. The problem wasn't that things were inconsistent. The problem was that there was nothing to be consistent with.

---

### Reframing the problem

The turning point came when I stopped looking at the audit findings and started looking at the product's category position.

Pocket FM is an audio-first entertainment platform. That sounds obvious, but it has a design implication the product had never really confronted: the interface was built as if it were a visual-first product. It borrowed heavily from video streaming, music apps and social entertainment — categories where the primary medium is something you see.

But audio is not something you see. Audio is something you feel. It exists in time, not space. It has rhythm, texture, weight and presence. And a platform built on audio was presenting itself through an interface that communicated none of that. This was not just a missed aesthetic opportunity. It was a strategic one.

> **"The problem I needed to solve was not visual inconsistency. It was identity."**

## Part 02 — The Strategic Bet

### Why iteration wasn't enough

Before I arrived, the approach to improving design had been iterative. Small component changes. Above-the-fold adjustments. Copy tweaks. Navigation refinements. This is a reasonable approach when the gap between current and desired state is a matter of degree.

But the gap here wasn't a matter of degree. It was structural. The product needed a new foundation, not better furniture.

At Pocket FM, the scale of the solution needed to match the scale of the problem. We were a business in the middle of a significant transformation — moving from India-first to global, from audio pioneer to category leader, from a product people used to a product people loved. Incremental improvements were not going to close that gap.

### Innovation, not variation

I made the case internally for a bigger bet: a new design system, built from first principles, that encoded not just what the product looked like but how it felt. A system capable of carrying the identity of the platform, not just governing its components.

The central question that shaped everything that followed:

> **"How could the design system evolve beyond governance and become a carrier of product identity itself?"**

---

### A single universal idea

Every design system needs a conceptual foundation — a central idea that makes decisions easier, keeps disparate elements coherent and gives people who work within it a shared sense of direction.

For Pocket FM, that idea came from the product's own nature: how do we visualise sound in a digital space? Audio has been visualised in many ways throughout history — spectrographs turn frequency into form, waveforms turn time into shape, physical instruments have their own visual vocabulary. These are systems of translating the invisible, the heard, into something seen.

That became the generative idea behind Aural: a design language that doesn't merely sit alongside the audio experience, but expresses it.

## Part 03 — Building Aural

### The creative process

The early explorations of what Aural's visual language could look like were, by any fair assessment, not good. They were overworked, derivative, trying too hard to be clever. We spent weeks on directions we eventually discarded entirely. This is not a failure of process. It is the process.

Great design work is not a flash of inspiration followed by execution. It is a long argument with the material — making things, looking at them honestly, deciding they're wrong, understanding why they're wrong, and making something closer to right.

### Typography: letters that carry memory

The typographic system didn't begin with a font search. It began with a question: what does type look like when it remembers audio?

We looked at the visual vocabulary of audio instruments across history — the elegant curves of wind instruments, the mechanical precision of early recording equipment, the vernacular lettering on vintage audio devices from the 1950s through the 1980s. Not for nostalgia, but to find letterforms that carried the right visual weight — that felt like they belonged in the same world as sound.

For functional text — labels, body copy, transactional information — we paired this with Lab Grotesque, a typeface with exceptional support for over 100 languages. This was non-negotiable. The design system had to be as capable in Hindi as in English, as coherent in Japanese as in Portuguese.

### Building blocks from the spectrograph

The structural visual elements of Aural — shapes, patterns, grids and component primitives — are derived from spectrography. A spectrograph translates audio into visual form: frequency on one axis, time on the other, amplitude expressed through intensity. It is the scientific representation of sound made visible.

We used this as the geometric grammar of Aural. The proportional relationships between elements, the way components stack and relate to each other, the rhythm of spacing — all carry the influence of spectrographic form. The idea is embedded in the structure, not applied as decoration.

### Motion: making the system breathe

An audio platform is not a static medium. Sound moves. It exists in time. It breathes.

The Aural system encodes this in its motion language. Transitions, micro-interactions and state changes are designed to carry the kinetic character of audio — not in a literal visualiser-style way, but in their timing, easing curves and relationship to user action. When you tap play and the interface responds, it should feel like something starting.

Motion in most products is an afterthought. In Aural, motion is part of the identity. It is one of the primary ways the product communicates what it is.

### Accessibility as a constraint, not a compromise

From the beginning, accessibility was a design constraint that shaped the creative work — not a layer added after the fact. A platform serving 200 million users has a responsibility to work for all of them. And designing for accessibility consistently produces better outcomes for everyone: the constraints force clarity, improve contrast, simplify interaction models and reduce cognitive load.

Aural meets and exceeds the latest WCAG standards. This was achieved without sacrificing the visual character of the system. Every part of Pocket FM is designed to work the same way for everyone.

### Global by design, local in feeling

Pocket FM serves users across multiple countries and cultures. The typographic system handles over 100 languages without degrading. The visual language was developed with international references rather than a single cultural vantage point. The component system was built to accommodate text expansion and contraction, different reading rhythms and varying navigation conventions across markets.

The goal was not a design system that felt neutral globally. Neutral is another word for forgettable. The goal was a design system that felt coherent everywhere — one Pocket FM, showing up fully in every market.

## Part 04 — Executing at Scale

### Why a system was the only answer

The scale of Pocket FM's product is significant. Hundreds of screens. Multiple platforms. Markets with different requirements. A team structure built around autonomous product squads, each moving at their own pace.

There was no version of this transformation that didn't go through a system. Redesigning individual screens one at a time would have taken years and produced a product that was always partially transformed — with new and old coexisting indefinitely. That half-transformed state can be worse than the original problem.

Aural was documented in a single location, accessible to designers, developers and product managers across the organisation. Every decision was explained not just as a rule but as a reason. People working within the system understood why it was built the way it was, which meant they could make good decisions in situations the documentation hadn't anticipated.

> 📷 **[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER]**
> *Aural documentation — system documentation page showing token structure, component library and usage principles.*

---

### Shipping early, publicly

One of the most important decisions in the execution of Aural was to turn it on internally as early as possible. Design system rollouts often happen in secret — built for months, then revealed. The problem with secrecy is that it produces distance.

We did the opposite. As soon as Aural was coherent enough to demonstrate, we made it visible inside the organisation. Week by week, surface by surface, people could see the product transforming.

> **"No one wanted their surface to be the one that looked outdated. The motivation to adopt was organic."**

This created pull rather than push. Teams weren't being told to adopt a new system — they were choosing to because they could see the outcome and they wanted to be part of it. You can't mandate adoption. You can make adoption desirable.

---

### Storytelling as design work

The most technically excellent design system in the world fails if the organisation doesn't understand why it exists. I spent a significant part of this project on communication: writing documentation, presenting to different parts of the organisation, framing the project's goals in terms that meant something to engineers, product managers, business stakeholders and content teams.

This is not a distraction from design work. It is design work. Strategy is storytelling. I learned early to speak in the listener's language — consistency and implementation time for engineers; retention and market differentiation for product managers; the relationship between great audio and great design for content teams.

The idea didn't change. The framing did.

## Part 05 — What Aural Changed

### From a functional product to a recognisable one

The most significant outcome of Aural is not measurable in any single metric. It's the shift in how the product feels — which is to say, how users relate to it.

Before Aural, Pocket FM was a product you used. It did its job. But it didn't have a feeling. It didn't have a personality that stayed with you after you closed the app. After Aural, the spectrograph-derived geometry, the instrument-influenced typography, the kinetic motion language and the accessible color system work together to create an experience that is not interchangeable with its competitors.

> 📷 **[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER]**
> *Before and after — side-by-side comparison of home, player and discovery surfaces before and after Aural.*

---

### A system that enables global expansion

Pocket FM was in the middle of a significant geographic expansion when Aural was built. The typographic system's 100+ language support, the culturally informed visual references, the accessibility compliance that works across different assistive technology conventions globally — all of these mean that the product can enter new markets without requiring a new design language for each one.

Many design systems are implicitly mono-cultural — built for one market, extended to others as an afterthought. The friction this creates is real: interfaces that feel slightly wrong, typography that strains at the edges of its script support. Aural was built to avoid that. Every market gets the full system.

---

### A framework that empowers teams

Before the system, design decisions at the component level required frequent alignment. Teams would build custom solutions to common problems, creating divergence that then needed to be reconciled. The cost was distributed across hundreds of small interactions that never showed up as a line item anywhere.

After Aural, teams have a shared language. When a designer and an engineer discuss a component, they're talking about the same thing in the same terms. The system doesn't eliminate design judgment — it makes design judgment more productive by eliminating the decisions that don't need to be made again.

---

### What the category now has

Aural exists in a context that matters: audio series is an emerging category with no established visual language. Every platform in this space has, to varying degrees, borrowed visual conventions from more established categories. Nobody had built the visual language of audio series from first principles.

> **"Not: how do we look like a great entertainment platform. But: how do we look like what we actually are."**

## Reflections — What I Learned

**Bigger problems require bigger solutions**
When the scale of what you're trying to change is large, the scale of your solution needs to match. Iterative improvement is valuable in many contexts. But there are moments — in a business's evolution, in a product's maturity — when only a larger commitment will do. The ability to recognise those moments and argue for the bigger bet is a leadership skill, not just a design one.

**Design systems are cultural artefacts**
The best design systems don't just govern components. They carry the values, decisions and point of view of the people who built them. Aural is not a neutral tool. It has an argument inside it — about what audio-first design should feel like, how accessibility works in practice, how a globally operating platform can be coherent without being homogeneous.

**Make fast, edit slow**
The creative process behind Aural involved a very large number of wrong answers before arriving at the right ones. The wrong answers are not waste — they're how you understand the problem deeply enough to solve it. The skill is in making quickly and editing honestly, not in being right on the first attempt.

**Storytelling is a design competency**
How you communicate a design vision across an organisation is not separate from the vision itself. The story you tell — to engineers, product managers, leadership and the organisation as a whole — determines whether the work happens or doesn't. I've become a better designer by becoming a better storyteller.

**New categories deserve new design thinking**
The temptation when building a product in an emerging category is to borrow conventions from adjacent categories because they're familiar and tested. That temptation is worth resisting. Borrowed conventions are someone else's answer to someone else's question. Aural is Pocket FM's own answer, built from its own material.

---

## Closing

Looking back at Aural, what I'm most proud of is not any individual element — not the typography, or the motion language, or the accessibility achievement, though I'm proud of all of those. What I'm most proud of is that we asked a harder question than we needed to and answered it seriously.

We could have done a visual refresh. Cleaned up the components, standardised the color tokens, written a tone-of-voice guide. It would have been faster, less risky, easier to explain. The product would have looked better. But it still wouldn't have felt like anything in particular.

> **"Aural is an attempt to make a product feel like what it actually is — which is one of the hardest things a designer can try to do, and the thing most worth trying."**
